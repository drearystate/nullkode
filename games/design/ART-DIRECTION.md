# ART DIRECTION STANDARD (owner's brief, 2026-10-06 — applies to every game the Game Studio builds)

Treat the game request above as the brief. Deliver a cohesive, visually finished game, with art direction appropriate to its genre, mechanics, and mood. Do not settle for a functional prototype with decorative assets added afterward.

ART DIRECTION
Before implementing, establish a concise internal visual specification: camera, palette, shape language, materials, lighting, environment density, interface style, and motion. Make deliberate choices suited to THIS game, and apply them consistently.
Derive these choices from the brief. Do not ask the person to make routine art direction decisions. Do not default every game to the same aesthetic.

ASSET COHERENCE
Choose assets that belong together. When using Kenney, KayKit, or other libraries, inspect what is actually available and select compatible subsets. Normalize scale, orientation, colors, and material treatment. Shared authorship does not automatically make every pack compatible.
Use recognizable, finished assets for important visible elements. Use primitives where they fit the chosen visual language, rather than as unexplained placeholders.

COMPOSITION AND DETAIL
Design what the player sees as carefully as how the game works.
Establish a clear visual hierarchy: gameplay focus, supporting environment, background, and interface. Make the initial view immediately readable and visually compelling.
Create detail through purposeful relationships, layering, and controlled variation. Group objects according to their function. Balance large forms, medium details, and small accents at the actual gameplay scale.
Avoid arbitrary prop scattering, uniform spacing, accidental empty areas, and clutter that obscures gameplay. Use negative space intentionally. Match detail density to the game; minimal games should feel intentionally minimal.

RENDERING
Make camera, lighting, materials, and effects work together.
For 3D, prioritize consistent scale, grounded objects, appropriate shadows, and readable silhouettes.
For 2D, prioritize consistent resolution, line weight, layering, animation, and clean edges.
Use effects to reinforce the art direction and communicate gameplay. Do not use bloom, particles, blur, or screen shake to disguise an unfinished scene.

INTERFACE AND FEEDBACK
Design the HUD, menus, icons, typography, and controls as part of the same visual system. Maintain consistent spacing, alignment, contrast, and sizing. Avoid generic interface styling unrelated to the game.
Give important actions immediate, proportionate feedback through animation, visual changes, and sound where available. Make selection, success, damage, failure, and state changes easy to understand.

SCOPE AND EXECUTION
Prefer a smaller, thoroughly finished experience over a larger, visually inconsistent one, while preserving the requested mechanics. Build a representative playable section early and resolve its visual language before extending it.
The first delivered version should already include the visual polish. Do not defer art direction, layout, animation, or feedback to a hypothetical later pass.

SELF-REVIEW
Before delivery, run the game and inspect its rendered output using available tools. No user-provided reference image is required.
Evaluate it against your visual specification:
- Does everything appear to belong to the same game?
- Is the gameplay focus immediately clear?
- Is the composition intentional at the actual play resolution?
- Are detail and empty space balanced appropriately?
- Do interface and motion match the environment?
- Are there placeholders, broken assets, awkward intersections, unreadable elements, or inconsistent scales?
Fix the most consequential visible problems and inspect again. Successful compilation alone does not establish visual quality. If you cannot inspect the rendered result, disclose that limitation.

Make the result feel authored, cohesive, and ready to demonstrate.
